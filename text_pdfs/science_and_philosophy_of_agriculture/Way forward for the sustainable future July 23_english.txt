Summery
Agriculture presents significant opportunities, particularly in Punjab where natural resources contribute
to a surplus of food. The future of the region depends on producing exportable surplus from agriculture
to address the trade deficit, stabilize food prices, and engage the youth productively. Disasters such as
soil and ocean degeneration, water scarcity, climate change, nutritional values of farm produce, food
sovereignty, and poverty are all interconnected. The global collapse of agricultural production systems
can be attributed to the excessive use of purchased inputs and the failure of current production systems.
At a crossroad of modernization, two options for future agriculture emerged: evolved tillage-based
practices and studying the natural ecosystem for sustainable production.
The understanding of the natural algorithm of soil fertility and vegetation renders existing practices
obsolete. Shifting towards sustained natural algorithm, known as PQNK, is necessary and has shown
cost reduction and increased yield and quality of produce among practicing farmers. Agriculture has the
potential to create jobs and income at the grassroots level, making it crucial to provide skills training
for youth to earn a decent living. By leveraging knowledge and resources, productivity can be
enhanced in the agricultural sector.
The One Acre Prosperity Model has the potential to revolutionize the agricultural sector. In many
cases, farm land is divided among family members due to inheritance laws, leaving small-scale farmers
with limited knowledge and resources to make a decent living. These farmers often rely on the
government for support in areas such as health, education, and job opportunities. However, with proper
training, they can be empowered to earn a sustainable income from just one acre of land, without
relying on government assistance.
To achieve this, a Crop Production Management Company (CPMC) should be established and
adequately funded. The CPMC would set up showcase training farms, production centers for
machinery, and crop production clusters. These initiatives would enable farmers to showcase their
produce and find various channels for distribution, aiming to reduce imports and create exportable
surpluses.
The outcomes of implementing such initiatives would include a sustainable trade surplus based on
domestic production, the creation of millions of self-employed jobs, poverty alleviation, wealth
creation at the grassroots level, retention of youth in rural areas, provision of natural and high-quality
food to the masses at reduced prices, water conservation (with potential savings of over 70%), and the
absorption and retention of CO2 in the soil through zero tillage practices, contributing to environmental
mitigation efforts. This initiative will play a key role in ensuring consistent supplies of nutrition-dense
food, dairy, and poultry products, as well as industrial raw materials, to both domestic and international
consumers and processors.

Disasters interrelate
The introduction and implementation of the Green Revolution in the late 1950s brought about
significant changes in agricultural practices. However, these changes, which relied heavily on the
intensive use of purchased inputs such as farm machinery and agrochemicals, disrupted the natural
ecosystem that had sustained life on Earth for billions of years. This disruption has led to various
interconnected and interdependent consequences, including:
Degradation of soil: The intensive use of agrochemicals and excessive water application has
led to a reduction in soil fertility and increased pH levels. This, in turn, hampers water
absorption capacity and requires frequent irrigations, resulting in mineral accumulation and
water wastage.
Excessive CO2 in oceans and atmosphere: The disruption of natural
ecosystem processes has contributed to the excessive release of
carbon dioxide into the oceans and atmosphere, exacerbating climate
change.
Extermination of lifeforms: The disturbance and inundation of soil
have resulted in the destruction of networks of beneficial organisms
such as mycorrhizal fungi and microbes. These organisms play a
vital role in converting minerals into a sustainable form and
providing balanced nutrition to plants, ultimately promoting healthy
growth and natural defense against pests, diseases, and
Weeds.
Water scarcity: The inefficient water management practices associated with
the current agricultural production system, including excessive water
application and poor water absorption, contribute to water scarcity issues.
Climate devastation: The disruption of natural ecosystem processes and the
release of greenhouse gases from agricultural activities contribute to climate
change, leading to devastating consequences such as extreme weather events,
rising temperatures, and altered precipitation patterns.
Food sovereignty: The current production system, with its heavy reliance on
purchased inputs, has undermined food sovereignty by making farmers
dependent on external inputs and market forces.
Nutritional values of farm produce: The reliance on intensive production
practices has often resulted in farm produce with lower nutritional density and

Poverty: The high cost of production associated with the current agricultural system, along with

Solution to protect soil from inundation
Protecting the soil from inundation is crucial for maintaining a healthy ecosystem. In nature, upland
areas are meant for vegetation, while lowlands provide
habitat for aquatic life. Inundation disrupts the balance
of the ecosystem, as fertile soil and plants are not
designed to thrive under water.
However, there are marshlands and swamplands that
naturally experience submersion during the monsoon
season. In these areas, certain plants and animals have
adapted to survive in such conditions. Nevertheless,
inundation is not the ideal situation even for these
species. Aquatic animals prefer to live in water, while
plants thrive in moist soil conditions.
To overcome the challenge of inundation, a system was
developed that goes along the natural ecosystem and
the algorithm designed for vegetation. Within the field,
a combination of raised beds and furrows is created.
The raised beds serve as the planting area for crops,
while the furrows help maintain soil moisture and
provide a passage for tractors. These beds are
permanent structures, allowing for the successive
planting and harvesting of crops without disturbing the
soil. This innovative system not only conserves water
but also shows significant water savings of over 70% in
the first crop cycle on the beds, and over 90% when the
beds are well covered with organic mulch.

Learning from the natural landscape, we implemented
several sustainable practices in crop production to
protect the soil and plants from inundation. These practices include the use of raised beds, which
elevate the planting area and prevent waterlogging, as well as the adoption of no-till planting
techniques to minimize soil disturbance. Additionally, soil mulching is employed to retain moisture and
suppress weed growth. Further, organic mulch serves as an insulation to moderate soil temperature. We
also emphasize controlled traffic, reducing the compaction of soil by limiting vehicle movement to
furrows only. Moreover, we recognize the importance of soil biota for plant nutrition, promoting a
reliance on natural soil organisms to enhance fertility and nutrient cycling. These combined strategies
contribute to a more resilient and sustainable agricultural system.

The prevailing agricultural practices have caused
immense harm to the natural ecosystem that had
thrived for billions of years, nurturing all forms of life
on our planet. The act of ploughing, in particular, has
disrupted the delicate balance of the soil's recycling
chain. It not only releases the long-stored stable soil
carbon in the form of CO2 but also decimates the
vibrant life teeming within it.
It is fascinating to contemplate that a mere teaspoon of
soil harbors more than seven billion microbes, acting as nurturing mothers to plants and providing them
with a harmonious blend of essential minerals necessary for their growth and resilience. These
microscopic organisms play an indispensable role in sustaining the health and vitality of our plant life.
Why do we plough soil?
The primary purpose of ploughing the soil is to ensure optimal conditions for seed germination. Seeds
require two essential elements to sprout successfully: warmth and moisture. While the sun provides the
necessary warmth, the moisture is obtained from the soil. To facilitate effective seed-to-soil contact and
enhance germination, the soil is pulverized or loosened.
However, it is crucial to acknowledge that this practice has been taken to an extreme level. Instead of
excessively pulverizing the entire field, a more balanced approach would involve loosening a narrow
strip of soil where the seeds can be planted. Advanced planting systems have been developed to
address this issue, ensuring that seeds can germinate efficiently by utilizing liquid or vapor moisture
techniques. These innovative methods aim to strike a balance between providing optimal conditions for
seed germination while minimizing unnecessary soil disturbance.

Abundance of Minerals in soil
The conventional belief is that crop plants deplete minerals from the soil, necessitating the addition of
an equivalent volume of minerals to maintain yield levels. This notion holds true for our current
production system, which has inadvertently disrupted the crucial role of soil microbes responsible for
converting soil minerals into a sustainable form readily accessible to plants.
Sparks, a reputable UK-based organization, has conducted extensive research by collecting samples
from Earth's crust and subjecting them to laboratory analysis. Their findings reveal a remarkable fact:
there isn't a single location where an abundance of minerals is not available. The only caveat is that
these minerals exist in a sustainable form that is not readily soluble in water, making them inaccessible
for plant uptake.
The table below showcases Sparks' data, illustrating the abundance of minerals in the soil. For instance,
every soil sample contains 2,000 parts per million (PPM) of nitrogen, whereas plants only require three
PPM. Similarly, phosphate levels reach 800 PPM in the soil, while plants need less than one PPM.
This data clearly indicates that the available minerals in the soil are more than sufficient to sustain
vigorous vegetation for billions of years to come. It emphasizes the importance of preserving and
restoring the soil's natural processes to ensure the optimal utilization of these abundant mineral
resources. Available minerals in soil are sufficient for next billions of years of vigorous vegetation.

SOIL LIFE - Nutrient Pools in Soil
In the natural algorithm of vegetation, soil microbes play a vital role in converting minerals into a form
that is readily available for plants. This process involves the transformation of minerals from a
sustainable, non-water-soluble state to a form that plants can efficiently uptake and utilize.
Soil microbes, such as bacteria, fungi, and other microorganisms, possess the remarkable ability to
break down organic matter and release nutrients in a plant-friendly form. They form symbiotic
relationships with plant roots, forming intricate networks like mycorrhizal fungi that enhance nutrient
absorption.
Through their metabolic activities, soil microbes contribute to the creation of nutrient pools within the
soil. These nutrient pools consist of essential elements like nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium, calcium,
magnesium, and various micronutrients. The microbes transform these elements into accessible forms,
ensuring a constant supply of nutrients to support plant growth and development.
The nutrient pools in soil act as a reservoir of essential elements, continuously cycling and replenishing
the soil's fertility. This natural process sustains the intricate balance of nutrient availability, facilitating
the healthy growth of vegetation.
Understanding and nurturing soil life, including the diverse community of microbes, is crucial for
maintaining the nutrient dynamics in the soil. By promoting practices that support soil microbial
activity, such as minimizing soil disturbance, incorporating organic matter, and avoiding excessive use
of agrochemicals, we can harness the power of soil life to enhance nutrient cycling and create a
thriving ecosystem for plants.

Seed: Unlocking Potential with Minimal Inputs
The seed, much like a car's speedometer, represents
potential. To fully tap into this potential, optimal
growing conditions are essential for the plant to
harness its vigor. When considering an entire acre of
land, the collective potential of all the seeds and
plants represents the production capacity.
A successful production process maximizes the
utilization of this potential capacity while
minimizing the need for purchased inputs. A good
process strives for the best conversion ratios,
ensuring efficient resource utilization. Additionally,
it aims to produce high-quality, uniform products
that can command premium prices in the market.
Unfortunately, the current prevailing crop production
processes were developed without a deep
understanding of the natural algorithm of vegetation.
The introduction of tractors, fertilizers, and pesticides under the guise of the Green Revolution
disrupted the natural balance. This process heavily relies on excessive use of purchased inputs,
deviating from the principles of optimal utilization.
In any system where a material undergoes a transformation, a process is involved. A superior process
leads to higher yields, reduced input requirements, and improved product quality. Optimal utilization
has never been a consideration in the current paradigm, but it is an essential aspect to strive for.
By embracing a production process that aligns with the natural algorithm of soil fertility and
vegetation, we can unlock the full potential of seeds while minimizing reliance on external inputs. This
approach not only maximizes productivity but also promotes sustainability, as it leverages the inherent
capabilities of the natural ecosystem. It is through such a process that we can achieve greater yields,

Quality certifications
Consumers are getting health conscious, they source eatables from a reliable sources to insure quality

In order for us to earn a meaningful slot in the international market, we are required to earn these
certifications, each of which has its own list of protocols.
Exporting agricultural produce to the European Union (EU) requires compliance with specific
certification and regulations. Here are some of the key certifications and requirements for agricultural
exports to the EU:
1. Plant Health Certificate: A Plant Health Certificate is required for the export of plant-based
agricultural produce. It ensures that the products are free from pests, diseases, and other contaminants.
The certificate is issued by the competent authority in the exporting country.
2. Phytosanitary Certificate: A Phytosanitary Certificate is another essential requirement for exporting
plants, plant products, and certain types of fresh produce to the EU. It confirms that the products have
been inspected and meet the EU's phytosanitary standards.
3. Good Agricultural Practices (GAP): Compliance with Good Agricultural Practices is increasingly
important for export to the EU. GAP certification ensures that agricultural products are produced in a
manner that minimizes environmental impact, protects worker health and safety, and ensures food
safety and quality.
4. Organic Certification: If you are exporting organic agricultural products, you need to obtain organic
certification from an EU-approved certifying body. The certification ensures that the products meet the
EU's organic farming standards.
5. Food Safety Management System: Compliance with food safety management systems, such as
Hazard Analysis and Critical Control Points (HACCP), is crucial. It ensures that the exported products
are safe for consumption and meet the EU's food safety requirements.
e for c
6. Labeling and Packaging Requirements: The EU has specific labeling and packaging requirements for
agricultural products. These include accurate product labeling, allergen declarations, country of origin
labeling, and compliance with packaging materials regulations.
7. Residue Monitoring: The EU has strict regulations regarding pesticide residues in agricultural
products. Compliance with Maximum Residue Limits (MRLs) is essential. Exporters may need to
provide evidence of residue monitoring and compliance with MRLS.

Crop rotation and yearly cycle
Crop production is divided into high-volume, low-value crops and low-volume, high-value crops.
Production planning takes into consideration market requirements, available facilities, and skill sets. In
the following spreadsheet, there is a mix of both types, allowing us to experience the benefits of each
option. Additionally, crops can also be classified based on their storability. Grains are typically
sustainable, oilseeds are relatively perishable, while onions, garlic, and potatoes can be stored for
extended periods in a controlled environment. The shelf life of fresh fruits and vegetables can also be
extended using proper facilities, such as a Controlled Environment Store (CA store).
It is possible to harvest two to four or even more crops per acre per year. However, in these projections,
we have determined that during the first year of operation, we anticipate having 2.25 crops.
We have also developed another system called Circular Production (CP), which allows us to grow
certain crops year-round. For example, radishes can be ready for harvest in 40 days from sowing. In
this system, we plant as many seeds as we can harvest and handle on the 40th day. This way, we
continuously plant and harvest with a 40-day gap, enabling many farmers to obtain eight crops per acre
per year.
On average, we can plant and harvest 170,000 radishes per acre. When multiplied by eight, it amounts
